  • Mental ill health is one of the leading causes of disability in Australia, imposing substantial personal, social, and economic burden across the lifespan.
  • Despite this burden, many people who experience mental ill health don't access professional support.
  • Only 56% of Australian children and adolescents with a mental health problem seek help, and only 30% receive treatment from a psychologist or psychiatrist.
  • Access is similarly constrained for adults. There are practical barriers, like cost, long waitlists, geography, and workforce shortages; stigma-related barriers, like fear of being judged; and attitudinal barriers, like wanting to cope alone or doubting that treatment will help.

This study

Examined attitudes and behavioural intentions toward generative AI tools for mental health care.

  • Yet little is known about how potential end users actually perceive and evaluate these tools — including people considering them for their own mental health, and parents and carers considering them on behalf of a child.
  • And if social media is anything to go by, those evaluations may be poor.
  • So the present study set out to address this gap, by examining attitudes and behavioural intentions toward generative AI tools for mental health care.

  • So what might shape whether people accept these tools?
  • Our outcomes were attitudes towards AI tools for mental health care, and behavioural intentions to use them.
  • A natural starting point is the Technology Acceptance Model, which proposes that people adopt technology when they see it as useful and easy to use. Trust has often been added as an extension to these models.
  • But AI raises considerations that these models don't capture: worries about privacy, how much human oversight people want, and concerns about its environmental cost.
  • And finally, experience: whether people had used generative AI before, and whether they'd already used it for their mental health.

Cross-sectional survey, within-person comparison of tool types

Measures
  • Usefulness, ease of use and trust
  • Privacy, oversight and environmental concerns
  • Attitudes and behavioural intentions
  • Prior use of generative AI

All rated on 1–7 scales, analysed with linear mixed-effects models

  • We ran a cross-sectional online survey of 186 Australians.
  • Ninety-five were adults aged 16 and over reporting on their own mental health, and 91 were parents and carers reporting on a child aged 4 to 17.
  • We recruited through Prolific, social media, public noticeboards and email lists.
  • Everyone rated two types of tool: general-purpose AI like ChatGPT, and bespoke, clinician-approved tools built specifically for mental health. So each person gave us a within-person comparison.
  • For each tool, they rated usefulness, ease of use and trust; privacy, oversight and environmental concerns; and their attitudes and intentions to use it. We also asked about their prior use of generative AI.
  • Everything was on 1 to 7 scales, and we used linear mixed-effects models to separate differences between people from differences within the same person.

Who held the negative views?

Students who had never used AI for their mental health. Those who had sat around neutral.

  • Turns out, our student sample didn't exactly feel the same way.
  • When we asked 320 university students the same questions, attitudes and intentions both fell below neutral.
  • And that negativity sat with students who had never used AI for their mental health. Those who had were right around neutral.
