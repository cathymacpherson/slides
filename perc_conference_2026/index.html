But Will People Use It?

Little is known about how potential users actually evaluate generative AI tools for mental health care, and social media suggests those evaluations may be poor.

  • Yet little is known about how potential end users actually perceive and evaluate these tools.
  • And if social media is anything to go by, those evaluations may be poor. People worry about AI eroding our ability to think, and about its environmental cost.
  • Though others point to real benefits: less administrative burden for clinicians, and support that's available around the clock.
  • So we set out to examine attitudes and behavioural intentions toward generative AI tools for mental health care.
  • First, we compared general-purpose tools, like ChatGPT, with bespoke tools built for mental health.
  • And second, we compared adults considering these tools for their own mental health with parents and carers considering them on behalf of a child.

  • So what might shape whether people accept these tools?
  • Our outcomes were attitudes towards AI tools for mental health care, and behavioural intentions to use them.
  • A natural starting point is the Technology Acceptance Model, which proposes that people adopt technology when they see it as useful and easy to use. Trust has often been added as an extension to these models.
  • But AI raises considerations that these models don't explicitly capture: worries about privacy, how much human oversight people want, and concerns about its environmental cost.
  • Then experience: whether people had used generative AI before, and whether they'd already used it for their mental health.
  • And finally, demographics: age, gender and education.

  • We ran a cross-sectional online survey of 186 Australians.
  • Ninety-five were adults aged 16 and over reporting on their own mental health, and 91 were parents and carers reporting on a child aged 4 to 17.
  • We recruited through Prolific, social media, public noticeboards and email lists.
  • Everyone rated two types of tool: general-purpose AI like ChatGPT, and bespoke, clinician-approved tools built specifically for mental health. So each person gave us a within-person comparison.
  • For each tool, they rated usefulness, ease of use and trust; privacy, oversight and environmental concerns; and their attitudes and intentions to use it. We also asked about their prior use of generative AI.
  • Everything was on 1 to 7 scales, and we used linear mixed-effects models to separate differences between people from differences within the same person.
Results

Favourable Overall, With a Preference for Bespoke Tools

Comfort with bespoke tools
  • Before comparing tool types, we checked whether people were favourable towards AI in mental health care at all.
  • Averaged across both tool types, attitudes sat at 4.94 and intentions at 4.85 on the 1 to 7 scale — both reliably above the midpoint. Adults and parents didn't differ.
  • Split by tool type, both attitudes and intentions were higher for bespoke tools than general-purpose ones, and that difference was significant. The intervals overlap, but because each person rated both tools, the comparison is within-person.
  • Next, we compared the two tool types. Bespoke tools were rated as more useful and more trustworthy than general-purpose tools.
  • Interestingly, people also wanted more human oversight of bespoke tools. There were no differences in ease of use, privacy or environmental concerns.
  • The direct comparison told the same story: more than half of adults and parents said they'd be more comfortable with a bespoke tool.

  • We then asked what predicted attitudes and intentions.
  • Here, the filled points represent significant predictors.
  • We started by looking at between-person predictors and found that, in general, people who saw AI as more useful and more trustworthy held more positive attitudes and stronger intentions.
  • From here, we compared each person's rating of the two tools (i.e., within-person) and found that when someone saw one tool as more useful or trustworthy than the other, that was the tool they held more positive attitudes towards.
  • Privacy concerns only mattered within people: the tool someone was more worried about was the one they rated less positively.
  • And environmental concerns didn't predict either outcome once usefulness and trust were in the model.
Results

Prior Use, Not Demographics, Predicted Acceptance

p < .05n.s.
  • Finally, participant characteristics.
  • Prior use of generative AI for mental health was the strongest predictor: those who had used it held more positive attitudes and stronger intentions — around one scale point higher.
  • That applied to 58% of adults and a third of parents and carers.
  • More frequent use of generative AI, and confidence with digital technology, also predicted more favourable views.
  • But age, gender and education didn't predict either outcome.
  • We can't yet say whether experience drives acceptance or the reverse — but it's a promising place to start.
Discussion

What This Means

1

Bespoke tools carry a governance expectation

  • Trusted more than general-purpose tools
  • Yet expected to have more human oversight
  • The least regulated tools are the ones users least expect to be supervised
2

Acceptance rests on usefulness and trust

  • Familiar technology-acceptance drivers did most of the work
  • Usefulness and trust shaped judgements of each tool
  • AI-specific concerns, including environmental ones, added little
3

Experience, not demographics

  • Prior use was the strongest predictor
  • Direction is unclear: use may build acceptance, or the reverse
  • If experience builds acceptance, supported first use is a lever for uptake
Take-home

People will use AI for mental health care when it is useful, trustworthy and familiar.

  • So what does this mean?
  • First, bespoke tools were trusted more, but that didn't make oversight feel less necessary — people wanted more of it. Oversight seems to follow from what a tool is for: a tool built for mental health care is seen as doing clinical work. That creates a mismatch, because the general-purpose tools that are least regulated in practice are the ones people least expect to be supervised.
  • Second, acceptance rested on the same things that drive acceptance of any new technology — usefulness and ease of use — with trust working both between and within people. AI-specific concerns, including environmental ones, added little once those were accounted for. So demonstrating clear, tangible usefulness is likely to matter most.
  • Third, experience mattered far more than demographics. We can't yet say whether experience builds acceptance or the reverse, but if it's the former, supported first use becomes a genuine lever for uptake.
  • So the take-home: people will use AI for mental health care when it's useful, trustworthy and familiar.
  • One caveat: our sample was more educated and better off than the general population — and the people most likely to face barriers to care may be the ones least represented. Which brings me to a different group…
Results

Undergrad Psych Students Were Less Favourable

Community sample (adults and parents/carers, n = 186)University students (n = 342)
Open question

Experience helps, but doesn't close the gap: what makes students more sceptical?

  • We then asked the same questions of 342 university students.
  • Unlike the community sample, students sat below the scale midpoint: attitudes at 3.75 and intentions at 3.38.
  • Breaking the students down by prior use, that negativity was concentrated among students who hadn't used generative AI for their own mental health — their intentions sat at 2.67.
  • Those who had were slightly above the midpoint for attitudes and at it for intentions.
  • So once again, prior use was by far the strongest predictor of both attitudes and intentions.
  • But experience doesn't close the gap: even students who had used AI for their mental health only reached about the midpoint, still below the community sample as a whole. So what makes students more sceptical?
  • We can only speculate — and this is a descriptive comparison between two samples that also differ in age and recruitment. But a few possibilities: the climate on campus, where there's a lot of anti-AI discourse right now; psychology training, which may place particular value on the human therapeutic relationship; and access, since students already have free university counselling, so an AI alternative may feel less needed.
