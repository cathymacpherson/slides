Kavanagh et al. (2023); Clement et al. (2012)

  • Mental ill health is one of the leading causes of disability in Australia, imposing substantial personal, social, and economic burden across the lifespan.
  • Despite this burden, many people who experience mental ill health don't access professional support.
  • Only 56% of Australian children and adolescents with a mental health problem seek help, and only 30% receive treatment from a psychologist or psychiatrist.
  • Access is similarly constrained for adults. There are practical barriers, like cost, long waitlists, geography, and workforce shortages; stigma-related barriers, like fear of being judged; and attitudinal barriers, like wanting to cope alone or doubting that treatment will help.

Digital Tools

  • Emerged in response to these barriers.
  • A promising alternative to traditional assessment and treatment.
  • But often fixed-format: limited room for personalisation, clarification or multimodal input.

Generative AI

  • Recently proposed as a solution to these barriers.
  • A flexible and responsive option for care.

Cross et al. (2024)

  • Digital options for mental health care have emerged in response to these barriers, offering a promising alternative to traditional assessment and treatment.
  • Yet such tools often rely on a fixed format, which limits opportunities for personalisation, clarification, or multimodal input.
  • Generative AI has recently been proposed as a solution, offering a more flexible and responsive option for care.
Introduction

But Will People Use It?

  • Little is known about how potential end users perceive and evaluate these tools:
    • adults considering AI for their own mental health
    • parents and carers considering it for a child
  • And social media suggests those evaluations may be poor.
This study

Examined attitudes and behavioural intentions toward generative AI tools for mental health care.

  • Yet little is known about how potential end users actually perceive and evaluate these tools — including people considering them for their own mental health, and parents and carers considering them on behalf of a child.
  • And if social media is anything to go by, those evaluations may be poor.
  • So the present study set out to address this gap, by examining attitudes and behavioural intentions toward generative AI tools for mental health care.

Attitudes and intentions (1–7 scale)
University students (n = 320) Community sample (n = 186)
Who held the negative views?

Students who had never used AI for their mental health. Those who had sat around neutral.

  • Turns out, our student sample didn't exactly feel the same way.
  • When we asked 320 university students the same questions, attitudes and intentions both fell below neutral.
  • And that negativity sat with students who had never used AI for their mental health. Those who had were right around neutral.
