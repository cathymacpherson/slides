AI Has a PR Problem

Why this research matters

The Gap

  • Fewer than 1 in 3 receive evidence-based treatment.

Johnson et al., 2016

  • AI has a PR problem.
  • Spend five minutes on social media and you'll see comments like these.
  • Some of those concerns are valid. People are scared about data centres draining water and power, while others are concerned about the cognitive impact of tools such as ChatGPT on our ability to learn and function.
  • But look elsewhere and you'll also find headlines like these — highlighting the potential of AI to fill real and important gaps in our healthcare system.
  • And some of the biggest gaps are in mental health. In Australia, only about half of children with a mental health problem seek help, and fewer than one in three receive evidence-based treatment from a psychologist or psychiatrist.
  • In the last year, we've seen the potential for AI to help fill those gaps — including for kids struggling with anxiety.
  • But AI has a PR problem. And so if we build these tools, will people actually use them?

Not what the comments say

Usefulness and ease of use drove attitudes. Prior AI use mattered most.

  • We surveyed 186 Australians. Ninety-five were adults reporting on their own mental health, and 91 were parents and carers reporting on a child. Each person rated two kinds of tools: general-purpose AI like ChatGPT, and bespoke, clinician-approved tools built specifically for mental health.
  • We had three key findings. First, despite the PR problem, people were fairly open. Attitudes sat comfortably above the midpoint, and adults and parents responded almost identically.
  • Second, bespoke tools won. People rated them as more useful and more trustworthy. Surprisingly, people also wanted more human oversight of them. Trust didn't replace oversight. It came with it.
  • Third, the factors driving attitudes weren't the ones dominating the comments section. Usefulness and ease of use explained over 80% of the variance. Once we accounted for trust and usefulness, environmental concerns no longer predicted anything. The strongest personal predictor wasn't age, gender or education. It was whether someone had already used AI for mental health.

Take-Home Message

Implications

People will use AI for mental health care when it's useful, trustworthy and familiar.

  • So what does this mean for anyone building these tools?
  • Lead with usefulness, and show people clearly what the tool does for them. Build in human oversight, because users expect it, even for the tools they trust most. And create opportunities for supported first use. We can't yet say whether experience drives acceptance or the reverse, but it's a promising place to start.
  • AI might have a PR problem. But for the people who'd actually use these tools, the question isn't “is AI good or bad?” It's “will this help me, or my child?”
  • If we can show them the answer is yes, they're ready to listen.
