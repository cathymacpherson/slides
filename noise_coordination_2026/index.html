Indexing Communication Difficulty
In Background Noise

via interpersonal coordination

Cathy Macpherson
Postdoctoral Research Fellow
cathy.macpherson@mq.edu.au

  • Today I'll talk about how background noise shapes the way people coordinate during conversation.
  • And whether that coordination can tell us when communication is starting to break down.
The question

Coordination In Conversation

When people converse, they align their behaviour with one another, coordinating movement, speech, and physiology.

Core Social Process

Coordination is a fundamental feature of social exchange.

Under Pressure

In noisy places, speech is masked and communication breaks down more often.

Question

Does coordination shift as communication becomes more difficult?

  • When we talk with someone, we don't just exchange words — we align our behaviour with them.
  • We coordinate our movements, our speech, and even our physiology.
  • This interpersonal coordination is a fundamental feature of social exchange.
  • But conversation rarely happens in quiet rooms. Cafés, offices, and parties are full of competing noise.
  • Noise masks speech, and communication breaks down more often.
  • If coordination is so central to interaction, it follows that it should change when communication gets harder.
  • So the question for this talk: does coordination shift with background noise — and could it tell us when communication is struggling?
  • To answer that, we first need to be clear about what kind of coordination we're measuring.
What we measure

Coordination At Multiple Scales

Local

  • Moment-to-moment: do partners move in similar ways at similar times?
  • Measured with cross-recurrence analysis.

Global

  • Structure of variability: how is each person's movement organised over time?
  • Do partners share that structure? (complexity matching)
  • Coordination can be captured at multiple scales. Here we focus on two.
  • The first is local, or moment-to-moment: are two people moving in similar ways at similar times?
  • The shaded regions show moments where partners occupy similar movement states.
  • We quantify this with cross-recurrence analysis — how often, and for how long, partners revisit shared states.
  • The second is global. Here we zoom out and look at how each person's movement fluctuations are organised over time.
  • At one extreme, fluctuations are random, like white noise. At the other, they are rigid and persistent, like brown noise.
  • Healthy, adaptive behaviour tends to sit in between — pink, or fractal, variability.
  • We index this with a scaling exponent, alpha. Higher alpha means more rigid movement.
  • Complexity matching then asks whether partners share that structure, even if they aren't moving in sync moment to moment.
  • Capturing either scale in real conversation raises a practical problem: how do we record movement without disrupting the interaction?
How we measure: recording

From Sensors To Pose Estimation

Physical Sensors

  • Gold standard - attach directly to the body.
  • Capture precise movement from selected body parts.
  • Often expensive and time-consuming to set up.

Pose Estimation

  • Works from ordinary video or webcam footage.
  • Extracts keypoints across the whole body.
  • Preserves natural interaction contexts.
  • Historically, coordination research has relied on simple rhythmic tasks, such as swinging pendulums in time with a partner.
  • Part of the reason is measurement.
  • Physical sensors are the gold standard. They are precise, but they need to be attached to the body.
  • They often capture only a few body parts, and fuller setups are expensive and time-consuming.
  • They can also take something away from the naturalness of the interaction.
  • Computer vision has changed this.
  • Pose estimation tools such as OpenPose extract keypoints across the whole body from ordinary video — a webcam is enough.
  • That makes real conversation measurable. But pose data brings its own challenges.
How we measure: analysis

Pose Estimation Challenges

  • Challenge: Hundreds of samples, biased by camera angle/position.
  • Best practice guidelines: Didn't really exist.
  • Analysis framework: Step-by-step from acquisition and feature selection through to linear and recurrence analysis.
Related preprint

Sale, C., Macpherson, M. C., Patil, G., Miles, K., Kallen, R., Wallot, S., & Richardson, M. J. (2026). Nonlinear methods for analyzing pose in behavioral research.

  • Pose estimation gives you a lot of data — hundreds of keypoints per frame, for every person.
  • That data is biased by where the camera sits and how people are positioned, and keypoints drop out when tracking fails.
  • If we want to compare two people's movement fairly, we need to remove these biases first.
  • When we started, there weren't really any best practice guidelines for doing this.
  • So we wrote some: a step-by-step framework from recording through to recurrence analysis, with an online quick guide.
  • We then applied this pipeline to a study of conversation in background noise.
The study

Conversation In Noise

  • Dataset: conversations from the MOSAIC dataset.
  • Participants: 47 pairs of friends, seated across a table.
  • Noise: realistic scenes through open headphones - office (60 dB), café (70 dB), food court (80 dB), party (87.5 dB).
  • Recording: 5-8 minute conversations, one webcam per person.
  • Breakdowns: listener repair requests, such as "what?" or "huh?".
  • The data come from the MOSAIC dataset.
  • 47 pairs — friends, couples, and family members — sat across a table and chatted freely.
  • They wore acoustically open headphones, so they could talk normally while hearing realistic recorded noise.
  • There were four noise scenes, from a quiet office at 60 dB through to a loud party at 87.5 dB.
  • Each conversation lasted 5 to 8 minutes, and each person was filmed with their own webcam.
  • We ran the videos through the pose pipeline to extract head, upper body, and arm movement.
  • To index communication difficulty, we coded repair requests: moments where the listener signals trouble, like "what?" or "huh?".
  • So we have movement at both scales, plus a measure of breakdowns. Let's start with local coordination.
Result 1: local coordination

Partners Coordinate More In Noise

Local Coordination

  • Arms and upper body: partners revisited shared movement states more often, and in longer, more structured sequences.
  • Face: more structured, but not more frequent.
  • Strongest: in the two loudest conditions.
  • We ran cross-recurrence analysis between partners for the face, upper body, and arms.
  • On the left is recurrence: as noise rose, partners' arms revisited shared movement states more often.
  • On the right is determinism: those shared states also unfolded in longer, more structured sequences.
  • The upper body showed the same pattern. The face became more structured, but not more frequent.
  • The biggest jumps were in the two loudest conditions — the food court and the party.
  • This replicates a previous study, where roughly 78 dB marked a critical point: past it, noise becomes much less manageable and behavioural dynamics shift.
  • So moment to moment, partners became more tightly coupled as communication got harder.
  • What about the global scale?
Result 2: global coordination

Movement Becomes More Rigid In Noise

Global Coordination

  • Individuals: head movement became more persistent and rigid (higher α) as noise rose.
  • Pairs: partners shared their movement structure beyond chance, but this didn't change with noise.
With motion sensors

When pairs stood rather than sat, complexity matching did increase with noise.

  • For the global measures, we tracked head movement: the midpoint between the temples.
  • Starting with individuals: alpha rose steadily with background noise.
  • Movement became more persistent and Brownian-like — more rigid, less flexible.
  • One interpretation is that people exert more deliberate control over their movement when communication is hard.
  • Turning to pairs: real partners showed more complexity matching than pseudo pairs — people who never actually talked.
  • So global coupling does emerge in conversation.
  • But unlike local coordination, it didn't change with noise.
  • One caveat: in an earlier study with motion sensors, pairs who were standing did show more complexity matching as noise rose.
  • So posture seems to shape which scale does the work — local coordination when seated, global coupling when standing.
  • So far we've looked at how these measures change with noise. But do any of them track communication actually breaking down?
Result 3: communication breakdowns

Rigid Movement Predicts Breakdowns

Communication Breakdowns

  • More rigid movement went with more repair requests, even after accounting for noise level.
  • How much people moved did not predict repairs.
  • Complexity matching did not predict repairs.
  • To find out, we related each measure to the number of repair requests.
  • The clearest result was at the individual level: more rigid movement went with more repair requests.
  • Importantly, this held even after accounting for noise level — so it isn't just noise driving both.
  • How much people moved didn't predict repairs. It's the structure of movement, not its size, that matters.
  • Complexity matching didn't predict repairs either.
  • This suggests that movement structure may serve as a behavioural marker of communication difficulty.
  • Let me pull this together.

Summary

  • Partners coordinate more, moment-to-moment, as noise rises.
  • Movement becomes more rigid, and rigidity tracks breakdowns.
  • Global coupling is present, but stable when seated.

Next Steps

Can we predict communication breakdowns in real time?

  • Back to the question: does coordination shift as communication gets harder? Yes — but differently at each scale.
  • Moment to moment, partners became more tightly coupled as noise rose.
  • Individually, movement became more rigid, and that rigidity tracked communication breakdowns.
  • Global coupling between partners was present, but stable when people were seated.
  • Together, individual and interpersonal dynamics seem to play complementary roles in keeping conversation going in noise.
  • And because all of this came from webcam footage, it opens the door to tracking communication difficulty outside the lab.
  • Which raises the next question: could we use these signals to predict breakdowns as they happen?
